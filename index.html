Key Concepts

This module covers the foundational concepts that underpin all protective operations. Every principle in this book is rooted in one insight: the attack happens now, and if you are not mentally present in the now, you cannot protect anyone.

⏱
Moment of Commitment
The instant an assailant takes an obvious action revealing intent — an action from which he cannot easily retreat. This starts the clock on the race between safety and lethality.
"There's an event before the sound of gunfire that actually marks the start of an attack: the Moment of Commitment. That's the instant in which an assailant first takes an obvious action likely to reveal intent, an action from which he cannot easily retreat. It might be his bringing a gun into view, or pushing forward through the crowd, jumping onto the stage, or raising his arm with a knife."
Just 2 Seconds, Chapter 1: "Now", p. 14
👁
Moment of Recognition
The instant protectors get their minds around what is happening — a prerequisite to getting their arms around the assailant or the protectee.
"Sometime (i.e., some time) after the Moment of Commitment comes the Moment of Recognition. This is the instant when protectors get their minds around what is happening, a prerequisite to getting their arms around what is happening — and more significantly, getting their arms around the assailant or the protectee."
Just 2 Seconds, Chapter 1: "Now", p. 14
⚡
The Big Response
The protector's decisive physical action — knocking the protectee to the ground or rushing them into the vehicle. Jerry Parr's Big Response on March 30, 1981, took roughly three-tenths of a second to initiate.
"By knowing the flavor of non-attack situations, good old Jerry Parr inversely knew the flavor of attack situations. He heard a pop, saw extreme movement in the periphery, and immediately his hands began to come up. By the second pop, he was already committed to completing an action... it took him about three tenths of a second to move through assessment of the sound, assessment of his other perceptions, the Moment of Recognition, and his commitment to the Big Response."
Just 2 Seconds, Chapter 1: "Now", pp. 26-27

The LADDER Framework

The training academy at Gavin de Becker Associates teaches the acronym LADDER to describe the elements required for effective protection:

L
Logistics
Ensure the ability to move without interference or delay. A protectee who is able to keep moving makes a more difficult target for attackers, and a less inviting target for those considering attack.
A
Advance
Have detailed knowledge of sites, routes, resources, emergency locations, schedules, known adversaries, likely occurrences; make this knowledge available to all who need it.
D
Distance
Establish and maintain the maximum practical distance from protectee to public; establish and maintain the minimum practical distance from protector to protectee; establish and maintain the minimum practical distance from protector to the closest members of the public.
D
Deterrence
Display protectors' highest readiness and effectiveness at all times.
E
Evacuation
Establish and maintain exit routes unblocked at all times.
R
Response
Maintain readiness and the highest ability to respond to attack or hazard at all times.
"LADDER serves as a checklist for protectors, a reminder to constantly confirm that each element of effective protection is in place. Though this book is about just the last rung in the ladder — Response to Attack — you'll see that studying it brings insights that are relevant to all other aspects of protection."
Just 2 Seconds, Chapter 1: "Now", p. 33

The Reagan Assassination Attempt — March 30, 1981

Washington Hilton Hotel, 2:25 PM • Compendium Section 4: Attacks Against U.S. Presidents
It is 2:25 PM on March 30, 1981. You are part of the Secret Service protective detail for President Reagan. The President is about to walk from the Washington Hilton Hotel to his limousine, approximately 30 feet away.

A group of people stands behind a slender cordon: reporters, photographers, onlookers. Among them — though you don't know it yet — is John Hinckley Jr., carrying a .22 caliber Rohm RG-14 revolver with six Devastator explosive-tipped rounds.

The President exits the building and waves to the crowd.
"A group of people behind a slender cordon is waiting to see President Reagan walk to his car. Some are waiting to get a closer look at him, some are waiting to photograph him, others are waiting to call out questions, and one is waiting to kill him."
Just 2 Seconds, Chapter 1: "Now", p. 13

Decision Point 1

You hear a sharp popping sound. What is your immediate action?

A
Wait for a second sound to confirm it's gunfire before acting
B
Immediately put hands on the President and begin moving him toward the vehicle
C
Draw your weapon and scan the crowd for the source
D
Push the President to the ground and cover him with your body
"At the instant Jerry Parr decided to put his hands on the President and start shoving him forward (no small decision, by the way), Hinckley was tracking Reagan at about stomach height. McCarthy caught the first bullet that had a good chance of hitting the President."
Just 2 Seconds, Chapter 1: "Now", p. 25
You've shoved the President into the limousine. Six shots have been fired in under two seconds. Press Secretary James Brady is on the ground, shot in the head. Officer Delahanty has been shot in the neck. Agent Tim McCarthy was hit while standing in the line of fire.

The President is in the back of the limousine. He says he feels fine but mentions "You're hurting me, Jerry." You run your hands under his suit — no blood. But then you notice red, bubbly blood in the President's mouth.
"Even though Parr observed no obvious problems, he ran his hands under the President's suit. No blood. But then Parr saw blood in the President's mouth. It was red, bubbly, and it instantly led Parr to his second Moment of Recognition for March 30th — and his second Big Response."
Just 2 Seconds, Chapter 1: "Now", p. 28
